\chapter{Optimal Sphere Packing in Dimensions 8 and 24}

\section{The Problem}

\subsection{Informal}
We all know how to stack oranges at the grocery store: one layer sits in the gaps of the layer below. In our everyday three-dimensional world, this is close to the best you can do. But mathematicians asked the same question about imaginary spaces with many more directions than three---spaces we cannot picture but can describe with equations. In 2016, Maryna Viazovska proved that in two specific higher-dimensional spaces, a certain beautifully symmetric arrangement of spheres is the absolute best possible packing. Her proofs were strikingly short compared to the enormous calculations needed for the three-dimensional case.

\subsection{Formal}
The problem is to determine the \emph{sphere packing constant} $\Delta_n$, the densest possible packing of unit spheres in $\mathbb{R}^n$. In dimension $n$, what is the maximum fraction of space that can be covered by non-overlapping spheres of equal radius?

\subsection{Historical Context}
After nearly 400 years since Kepler, the problem remained wide open in most dimensions. Maryna Viazovska's 2016 breakthrough solved it for two specific dimensions using an entirely new analytic method.

\subsection{What Was Known}
The problem had been solved only in dimensions 1, 2, and 3 before 2016. In dimension 3, Kepler's conjecture was proved by Hales in 1998 using computer-assisted methods. For higher dimensions, the best known bounds were far from optimal. The $E_8$ lattice and the Leech lattice were conjectured to be optimal in dimensions 8 and 24 respectively, but no proof existed.

\subsection{Why It Matters}
Sphere packing in higher dimensions is directly connected to error-correcting codes---the mathematical tools that keep digital communication reliable. The densest packings in dimensions 8 and 24 correspond to the best possible error-correcting codes, used in everything from cell phones to deep-space communication. Understanding these packings also reveals deep connections between number theory, geometry, and physics.

\subsection{Simplified Version}
In two dimensions, Thue proved in 1910 that the hexagonal lattice packs circles more densely than any arrangement---the density is $\pi/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.9069$. In three dimensions, the face-centered cubic lattice achieves density $\pi/\sqrt{18} \approx 0.7405$, and Hales proved in 2005 that this is optimal. The shock of Viazovska's work was that dimensions 8 and 24---far from the intuitive low-dimensional cases---have remarkably elegant optimal packings (the $E_8$ lattice and the Leech lattice), and these turn out to be optimal by a wide margin.

\section{Mathematical Theory}


\subsection{Prerequisites}
This chapter assumes familiarity with:
\begin{itemize}
\item Linear algebra (lattices, Gram matrices)
\item Fourier analysis (Poisson summation formula)
\item Modular forms (basic properties)
\item Convex geometry (packing density)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lattice Packings}

\begin{definition}[Lattice Packing]
A \emph{lattice packing} is a sphere packing where the centers form a lattice $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. The density is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(\Lambda) = \frac{\text{vol}(B_n(r))}{\det(\Lambda)} = \frac{\pi^{n/2} r^n}{\Gamma(n/2 + 1) \det(\Lambda)}
\end{equation}
where $2r$ is the minimal distance between lattice points.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Special Lattices}

Two lattices stand out in dimension theory:

\begin{definition}[$E_8$ Lattice]
The \emph{$E_8$ lattice} is the unique even unimodular lattice in dimension 8. It can be constructed as:
\begin{equation}
E_8 = \{(x_1, \ldots, x_8) \in \mathbb{Z}^8 \cup (\mathbb{Z} + \tfrac{1}{2})^8 : \sum x_i \equiv 0 \pmod{2}\}
\end{equation}
Its minimal norm is 2, and its kissing number is 240 \cite{conway1999}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Leech Lattice]
The \emph{Leech lattice} $\Lambda_{24}$ is the unique even unimodular lattice in dimension 24 with no vectors of norm 2. Its kissing number is 196,560 \cite{conway1999}.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The Cohn--Kumar Method}

In 2001, Henry Cohn and Abhinav Kumar showed that if a certain class of radial functions satisfied a pair of inequalities, then the $E_8$ and Leech lattice packings would be optimal. This approach, now called the \emph{Cohn--Kumar linear programming bound}, required constructing an auxiliary function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with specific properties \cite{cohn2003}.

\begin{theorem}[Cohn--Kumar, 2001 \cite{cohn2003}]
If there exists a radial function $f$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(x) \leq 0$ for $\|x\| \geq 1$ (no closer packing)
\item $\hat{f}(\xi) \leq 0$ for $0 < \|\xi\| \leq 1$ (Fourier condition)
\item $f(0) = \hat{f}(0)$
\end{enumerate}
then the lattice packing is optimal.
\end{theorem}

Cohn and Kumar (2001) constructed such functions for dimensions $n \leq 8$ and $n = 24$, but only for \emph{lattice} packings, not arbitrary packings.

\section{The Solution}

\subsection{The Big Idea}
Imagine you want to prove that a certain arrangement of objects in an 8-dimensional room is the absolute densest possible. Cohn and Kumar had shown in 2001 that this boils down to constructing a special ``witness function''---a mathematical certificate that, if you can build it, immediately proves optimality. The catch was that nobody could construct this witness exactly. Previous attempts used computer-searched approximations, which are like a witness who says ``I'm 99.99\% sure''---close, but not proof. Viazovska's stroke of genius was to realize that modular forms---ancient number-theoretic functions with extraordinary symmetry---already encode exactly the witness you need. The deep symmetries of modular forms automatically force the witness function to have the right shape at every point, not just at the points you checked.

\subsection{What Was Stopping Progress}
The Cohn--Kumar linear programming bound had already reduced the packing problem to an explicit construction task: find a radial function satisfying three specific inequalities. Previous attempts used numerically optimized functions---piecewise polynomial approximations checked on a computer grid. But a numerical check is not a proof: the inequalities could fail at untested points, and no exact analytic formula was known. It was like trying to prove a bridge can hold any weight by testing it with trucks up to 100 tons---you can never be sure an 101-ton truck wouldn't cause collapse.

\subsection{The Breakthrough}
Viazovska noticed that the $E_8$ lattice's theta series---the function that counts lattice vectors at each distance---is a modular form, a function with profound symmetry properties. Modular forms satisfy identities that relate their values at different points, automatically enforcing exactly the three inequalities Cohn and Kumar needed. By constructing the witness function as an integral transform of a carefully chosen modular form, Viazovska obtained an \emph{exact} proof---no approximations, no numerical checks. The modular symmetries do all the work, guaranteeing correctness at every point simultaneously. The proof was completed in a single weekend and spans only 20 pages.

\subsection{How It Works}

\subsection{What Was Missing}
The $E_8$ lattice (dimension 8) and the Leech lattice (dimension 24) had been conjectured optimal for decades, and Cohn--Kumar (2001) showed that if a certain auxiliary radial function satisfying three inequalities could be constructed, then optimality would follow. What was missing was an explicit construction of this function. Cohn and Kumar found numerically optimized candidates that worked for many lattice packings, but numerical approximations are not proofs: the inequalities could fail at untested points, and no exact analytic formula was known.

\subsection{Why Existing Methods Failed}
The Cohn--Kumar linear programming bound reduces the packing problem to constructing a radial function $f$ with $f(x) \leq 0$ for $\|x\| \geq 1$, $\hat{f}(\xi) \leq 0$ for $0 < \|\xi\| \leq 1$, and $f(0) = \hat{f}(0)$. Previous attempts used piecewise polynomial or numerically optimized functions, which could only verify the inequalities on a finite grid---leaving open the possibility that violations existed elsewhere. Moreover, extending these methods to prove optimality for \emph{arbitrary} (not just lattice) packings required a fundamentally different approach, because the Fourier-analytic framework for non-lattice packings is much less constrained.

\subsection{What Was Novel}
Viazovska's 2016 proof was revolutionary because she constructed the auxiliary function \emph{exactly} using \textbf{modular forms}. The theta series of the $E_8$ lattice is a modular form of weight 4 for $\Gamma_0(2)$, and Viazovska used this to build $f$ as an integral transform involving the Bessel function $J_4$ and a polynomial $P$ determined by modular form coefficients. The modular form identity guarantees the Fourier-analytic inequalities \emph{exactly} at all critical points simultaneously---no numerical approximation needed. The proof was only 20 pages and was completed in a single weekend.

\subsection{Student-Friendly Explanation}
Imagine you want to prove that stacking oranges in the $E_8$ pattern (in an 8-dimensional room) is the absolute best way to pack them. Cohn and Kumar showed that this boils down to constructing a special ``test function'' that certifies optimality---like a mathematical witness. Previous attempts found approximate witnesses by computer search, but an approximation is not a proof. Viazovska's insight was that modular forms---functions with deep symmetry properties from number theory---can be used to write down the witness \emph{exactly}. The symmetry of modular forms automatically forces the test function to have the right shape everywhere, not just at finitely many checked points.

\subsection{Viazovska's Breakthrough (2016)}

In February 2016, Maryna Viazovska, then at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, posted a paper on arXiv proving that the $E_8$ lattice gives the densest sphere packing in dimension 8 \cite{viazovska2017}:

\begin{theorem}[Viazovska, 2016 \cite{viazovska2017}]
The $E_8$ lattice achieves the maximal packing density among all sphere packings in $\mathbb{R}^8$. The optimal density is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_8 = \frac{\pi^4}{384} \approx 0.25367
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Viazovska's method was revolutionary:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Modular forms as auxiliary functions}: She constructed the required function $f$ explicitly using a specific modular form of weight 4 for $\Gamma_0(2)$:
\begin{equation}
f(r) = \int_0^\infty \left(\frac{J_4(\pi r t)}{(\pi r t)^4} - e^{-\pi r^2 t^2/4}\right) P(t) t^7 \, dt
\end{equation}
where $J_4$ is the Bessel function and $P$ is a polynomial determined by modular form coefficients \cite{viazovska2017}.
\item \textbf{Exact interpolation}: Unlike Cohn--Kumar who needed approximate inequalities, Viazovska's function satisfies the inequalities \emph{exactly} at the critical points (the norms appearing in the $E_8$ lattice).
\item \textbf{Radial Fourier transform}: She exploited the explicit formula for the radial Fourier transform in dimension 8, which simplifies dramatically due to the special properties of $E_8$.
\end{enumerate}

The proof was remarkably short (about 20 pages) compared to Hales's 250-page proof of the Kepler conjecture.

\subsection{Extension to Dimension 24 (2016)}

In April 2016, Viazovska, together with Henry Cohn, Abhinav Kumar, Stephen Miller, and Danylo Radchenko, extended the method to dimension 24 \cite{cohn2017}:

\begin{theorem}[Cohn--Viazovska--Kumar--Miller--Radchenko, 2016 \cite{cohn2017}]
The Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$ achieves the maximal packing density among all sphere packings in $\mathbb{R}^{24}$. The optimal density is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{24} = \frac{\pi^{12}}{12! \cdot 2^{12}} \approx 0.00193
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The dimension 24 proof required a more sophisticated auxiliary function involving a linear combination of modular forms for $\Gamma_0(4)$, and the polynomial $P$ had to satisfy 47 interpolation conditions \cite{cohn2017}.

\begin{highlightbox}
Viazovska's proof of the dimension 8 case was completed in a single weekend. She was inspired by the Cohn--Kumar method but found a way to construct the auxiliary function exactly using modular forms, bypassing the need for numerical optimization.
\end{highlightbox}

\subsection{Why Only Dimensions 8 and 24?}

The method relies crucially on the existence of even unimodular lattices, which only exist in dimensions divisible by 8. In those dimensions, the theta series of the lattice is a modular form, which provides the analytic structure needed to construct the auxiliary function.

For other dimensions, the problem remains open. However, the Cohn--Kumar method (2017) has been used to prove optimality for many additional dimensions (up to $n = 43$) using a variant of the approach, though not always with the same elegance as Viazovska's exact construction \cite{cohn2017b}.

\section{Impact}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fields Medal (2022)}: Viazovska was awarded the Fields Medal at the ICM in St. Petersburg (held virtually due to the war in Ukraine), partly for this work \cite{fields2022}.
\item \textbf{Modular forms in discrete geometry}: The connection between modular forms and sphere packing is now a major research area \cite{viazovska2017, cohn2017}.
\item \textbf{Dimension 12 and beyond}: Cohn and Kumar (2017) used numerical optimization of the Cohn--Kumar bound to prove optimality in dimensions 12, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, and 23 \cite{cohn2017b}.
\item \textbf{Connection to codes}: The $E_8$ lattice is related to the extended Golay code in dimension 24, and the sphere packing results have applications to coding theory.
\item \textbf{String theory}: The $E_8$ and Leech lattices appear in bosonic string theory and conformal field theory, connecting discrete geometry to physics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Basic]
Show that the kissing number of the $E_8$ lattice is 240. (Hint: Count the vectors of norm 2 in $E_8$.) \cite{conway1999}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Intermediate]
Prove that the density of the hexagonal lattice packing in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $\pi/\sqrt{12}$. \cite{thue1910}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge]
Explain why the Poisson summation formula is essential in the Cohn--Kumar method. How does it relate the primal problem (sphere packing) to the dual problem (Fourier analysis)? \cite{cohn2003}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
The theta series of a lattice $\Lambda$ is $\Theta_\Lambda(q) = \sum_{x \in \Lambda} q^{\|x\|^2/2}$. Show that for $E_8$, this is a modular form of weight 4 for $\Gamma_0(2)$. Why is this connection crucial for Viazovska's proof? \cite{viazovska2017}
\end{exercise}
